A day pass runs on the clock, not the calendar

“Buy the park pass when we arrive” sounds like a tidy job for the first afternoon. In Kosciuszko National Park it can cost you half a day: the vehicle pass runs for 24 hours from the moment you buy it, and it belongs to one number plate. Four checks before the pass goes into a family plan.

Most families treat a national park pass as a box to tick: pay at the gate, put the receipt on the dashboard, go. NSW National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) says most of its fee-collecting parks do charge by the calendar day. Kosciuszko, home to Australia’s highest mountain, is one of the exceptions, and the difference decides when you should buy.

Most parks count days. Kosciuszko counts hours.

NPWS says Kosciuszko National Park day passes are valid for a 24-hour period. Its conditions of use say a pass is valid from the time of purchase until the date and time shown when you buy it. Outside the winter peak, the fee is A$17 per vehicle per 24 hours; in the winter peak, from the June to the October long weekend, it is A$29.

So the clock starts at the counter, not at the gate. By our arithmetic, a pass bought at 4 pm on arrival day, to get it out of the way, runs out at 4 pm the next day, in the middle of a long walk. Bought at 8:30 am on the first morning the car goes in, the same pass covers the whole day.

The pass belongs to the plate

NPWS says the fee is for vehicles, not people: walkers, cyclists and public-transport passengers don’t need a day pass, while bus passengers pay a per-person fee. When you buy, you give the car’s registration plate, and NPWS says penalties apply to a vehicle without a valid pass matching its details, including through user error. Day passes can’t be moved to another car.

That matters most in a hire car. NPWS’s annual passes, including the A$190 All Parks Pass, aren’t valid for rental cars under its conditions of use. A family flying in and hiring a car is buying day or multi-day passes, and needs the hire car’s plate in hand first, which means after the pick-up, not at home the night before.

Where you pay, and how

NPWS sells day and multi-day passes at its vehicle entry stations during operating hours, at its local visitor centres, at the Thredbo Visitor Centre and at the Perisher Valley office. Entry stations take Visa and Mastercard, not cash; Snowy Region Visitor Centre in Jindabyne, Tumut and Khancoban visitor centres take cash.

NPWS’s Park’nPay app also sells passes in the Thredbo-Perisher area, but NPWS says mobile reception in the park is limited. Our inference: buy in town, where you have signal and a counter, rather than counting on the app in a car park. Snowy Region Visitor Centre sits outside the park, opens 8:30 am to 5 pm daily, and charges no entry fee to visit.

A valid pass is not an open road

The conditions of use say a valid pass doesn’t override gate closure times, local signage or directions from NPWS staff, and doesn’t guarantee access to any area or a parking space. Unused hours aren’t refunded. NPWS says tracks such as Pallaibo may close for poor weather, snow, fire danger or track damage, so a pass bought for a closed day is still spent.

Four checks before the pass goes in the plan

1. The clock. Note the hour you will first drive in, and buy then. Read the end time on the receipt, not the date.

2. The plate. Buy for the car you are driving, with its registration typed exactly. In a hire car, an annual pass isn’t an option.

3. The payment. Carry a Visa or Mastercard for entry stations, and buy where you have signal or a counter.

4. The road. Check NPWS alerts the morning you buy, so the 24 hours start on a day the walk is open.
