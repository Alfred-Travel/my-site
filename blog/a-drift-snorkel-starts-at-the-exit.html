A drift snorkel starts at the exit

On a reef snorkel from the beach, the plan is where you get out, not where you get in. Four checks to settle on the sand before a family gets wet: which part of the bay, what the current is doing, where the exit is and who swims with whom.

A beach day in a trip plan usually reads as a place and a time: Turquoise Bay, late morning, snorkel. At Ningaloo, in Western Australia, the park service treats the water itself as the decision. The same bay has a calmer snorkel and a drift snorkel, and the difference is a current you can’t see from the car park.

Two snorkels in one bay

The Parks and Wildlife Service (DBCA) page for Turquoise Bay, in Cape Range National Park near Exmouth, splits the bay in two. The best snorkelling, it says, is reached from the Bay Loop car park in the northern half of the bay, well away from the potentially strong currents at the sandy point to the south.

The Drift Loop car park serves the drift snorkel. DBCA says to walk about 200 metres south, enter the water at the southern end of the beach and let a natural south-to-north current take you over the coral. That current is the point of the drift, and it is also the hazard.

Why the exit matters more than the entry

DBCA says the strong currents at Turquoise Bay are usually found around the sandbar at the southern point of the Bay Loop and at the northern end of the Drift Loop. Water builds up in the lagoon in front of the reef and has to escape through gaps in it; large waves and high tides add to it. So the drift ends close to where the current can be strongest.

It also says park rangers don’t regularly patrol the beaches at Turquoise Bay, and its beach safety page says beaches in parks and reserves are almost all unpatrolled. If a child tires at the end of the drift, there may be nobody on the sand but your own family.

What the park asks of you

DBCA’s advice reads like a checklist. Assess the current from the beach and seek advice from people with sound local knowledge. Read the signs at the beach entry points. Never snorkel alone, and if in doubt, don’t go out. Inexperienced swimmers shouldn’t swim or snorkel in strong currents; if they can’t find an experienced person to go with them, DBCA suggests a licensed tour operator.

Its general snorkelling page adds two more: check entry and exit points, waves, currents, visibility and boats before going in, and use a suitable flotation device if you aren’t a confident swimmer. Its swimming page asks adults to actively supervise children, including those who have learnt to swim.

The same thinking runs along the coast. DBCA says Oyster Stacks should be snorkelled only at high tide, at least 1.2 metres, with the tide checked at Milyering Discovery Centre. At Lakeside, you snorkel between the beach and two yellow sanctuary buoys, a boundary the children can see.

Four checks before anyone gets wet

1. Site. Start with the calmer option. At Turquoise Bay that is the Bay Loop, not the drift. Save the drift for a later day, after the family has snorkelled the reef in easier water.

2. Current. Watch the water from the beach, read the sign at the entry point, and ask at Milyering what the current is doing that day. If it is running, stay on the sand or well inside the bay.

3. Exit. Before you go in, pick the spot on the beach where you will get out, and point it out to the children. On the drift, that is well before the sandy point at the northern end.

4. Buddy. Pair each child with an adult who swims with them the whole way, and give anyone who isn’t confident a flotation device. If no adult has snorkelled in a current before, book a licensed tour or skip the drift.
