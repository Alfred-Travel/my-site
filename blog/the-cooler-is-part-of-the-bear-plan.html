The cooler is part of the bear plan

In the Smokies, the bear plan starts when you pack the cooler, not when you see a bear. Four checks to settle before a family picnic or wildlife drive: how far away to stay, how to leave the table, what to do with the car and where to stop.

A day plan in a black bear park usually reads like a list of sights: the loop road, a waterfall, a picnic by the river. In Great Smoky Mountains National Park, the park service treats two of those as bear decisions. Where you stop to watch wildlife, and what you leave behind after lunch, decide whether a bear learns to approach people. Both are settled before the cooler goes in the car.

The rule: 50 yards, and it is the law

The National Park Service (NPS) says willfully approaching within 50 yards (150 feet, about 46 metres) of a bear, or any distance that disturbs or displaces it, is illegal in the park and can lead to fines and arrest. For elk, which live on the North Carolina side of the park, the distance is 25 yards, about 23 metres.

NPS also gives a test for “too close”. If a bear stops what it is doing, watches you or changes direction because of you, you are too close. Back away slowly while facing the bear, and never run.

Why the picnic matters

NPS says a black bear’s sense of smell is about seven times stronger than a bloodhound’s. Hot dogs, apple cores, chip crumbs and watermelon rinds left in picnic areas or beside trails can be deadly to bears. Once a bear gets human food, it loses its natural fear of people and becomes unpredictable. NPS says bears are put down in the Smokies almost every year.

Rangers strictly enforce the rules on littering, feeding wildlife and storing food. NPS says breaking them can mean a fine of up to US$5,000 and up to six months in jail.

What breaks when the plan only says “picnic”

A plan that says “Cades Cove, then lunch” leaves out the parts the park cares about. Its picnic page asks you to clean the picnic area after the meal, including the grill and the ground around the table. Some areas also change through the year: at Metcalf Bottoms, NPS removes the dumpsters from late October to late March, so you carry out every scrap.

The car is part of it too. NPS says to lock vehicles so bears can’t reach food or rubbish inside, because bears can open car doors.

A wildlife drive needs a stopping rule as well. NPS asks drivers to use roadside pullouts with all four tyres off the road and not to block traffic, and to go back to the car if an animal comes near. It says early mornings and late evenings are the peak times for animal activity, and suggests bringing binoculars.

Four checks before the cooler goes in the car

1. Distance. Agree the number with the children before anyone sees a bear: 50 yards, about 46 metres, and 25 yards for elk. Pack binoculars so nobody needs to get closer.

2. Picnic. Name the picnic area, check that it is open on your date, and bring bags to carry everything out. Leave the table, the grill and the ground clean, and use the bear-proof bins.

3. Car. Lock the car every time you walk away from it, even for a short walk. NPS says bears can open car doors.

4. Pullout. Stop to watch wildlife only in a pullout with all four tyres off the road. If an animal comes near, back away and get back in the car.
