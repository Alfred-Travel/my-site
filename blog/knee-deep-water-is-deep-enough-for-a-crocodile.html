Knee-deep water is deep enough for a crocodile

A calm billabong on a hot afternoon looks like an invitation. In Kakadu, the park’s own advice is that even wading can be dangerous and that the safest swim is in a pool, so the water plan belongs in the itinerary before the walk does.

Families in Australia’s tropical north meet the same scene again and again: still water, shade and a hot child. This note sets out what Parks Australia says about crocodiles and water in Kakadu National Park, what that means for a family day, and four checks to make before anyone goes near the edge.

What the park says

Parks Australia’s Kakadu safety page, read on 26 November 2026, says saltwater crocodiles are dangerous and have attacked and killed people. They live in most areas of water in the park, it says, including floodplains, freshwater billabongs, creeks, rivers and coastal areas. Always obey warning signs; if there are no warning signs, assume crocodiles are present.

On swimming, the page is direct. Crocodiles usually hunt by staying submerged and can attack in knee-deep water, so even wading can be dangerous. Some waterways are also prone to flash flooding and rapid currents. The safest place to swim in Kakadu, it says, is a public or hotel pool, and you should never swim alone.

The same page takes apart some common beliefs. Saltwater crocodiles can be found more than 200 km upriver in fresh water. They are often seen in fast-flowing water and on beaches, they have been known to travel up to a kilometre on land, and the dry season doesn’t make them less likely to attack.

Why it matters more with children

Parks Australia says children are at particular risk at the water’s edge, and that crocodiles can stay hidden underwater for long periods, so you won’t always see them. Our inference: water that is ankle-deep on an adult can be knee-deep on a seven-year-old, and the risky moment is often not a swim but a paddle, a dropped hat or a stone thrown from a low bank.

Heat makes the water more tempting. Parks Australia says temperatures in the park regularly reach 30°C or more and advises against walking between 2.30 pm and 6.30 pm. Our recommendation: decide where the hot part of the day will be spent, and which pool it includes, before you arrive, so nobody is improvising beside a billabong.

An open site is not a swimming invitation

The park’s daily access report shows how the risk moves. When we read it on 26 November 2026, Maguk was closed for the wet season after heavy rain, with fast-flowing, rising water “increasing crocodile risk”. Parks Australia lists crocodile trapping and surveys among the steps before a site reopens after the wet. Our inference: “open” tells you a site can be visited, not that its water is safe to enter.

Four checks before a day near water

1. The sign, or the assumption. Read every warning sign at the car park and the track head. Where there isn’t one, Parks Australia says to assume crocodiles are present.

2. The distance from the edge. Parks Australia’s camping advice is to avoid the water’s edge and do chores at least 50 metres away; for fishing, it says to stay at least 10 metres back, further if the bank is flat and low. Our suggestion for a family walk: keep children on the track side of an adult.

3. The pool for the hot hours. Know where the afternoon swim will be. Parks Australia lists a 50-metre public pool in Jabiru and a pool at Cooinda that non-guests are welcome to use.

4. The safe place to look. Crocodiles are for platforms and boats. Parks Australia names safe viewing platforms at Cahills Crossing and Yellow Water, and two cruises. At Cahills Crossing, it says not to cross the causeway on foot.
