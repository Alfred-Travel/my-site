Lightning keeps an afternoon timetable

On a summer mountain day, the sky at breakfast says little about the sky at two o’clock. At Rocky Mountain National Park, the park service and the weather service both say storms build in the afternoon, so the walk back belongs in the plan before the walk out.

Most family park days are planned around the walk: which lake, how far, where to eat. The weather gets a glance at breakfast. In Rocky Mountain National Park in Colorado, the National Park Service (NPS) says afternoon thunderstorms and wind are normal summer patterns on the alpine tundra. That makes the time of day part of the route, not a detail.

What the park and the weather service say

NPS’s high country hazards page, read on 25 November 2026, says a bright, sunny day can turn windy and wet within minutes. For lightning, it says to start walks early, planning to get below treeline or to a shelter before a storm strikes. If you are caught above treeline, it says to move away from summits and isolated trees and rocks, avoid small cave entrances and overhangs, and crouch down on your heels.

The National Weather Service (NWS) says there is no safe place outside when thunderstorms are in the area, and that if you can hear thunder you are probably within striking distance. The only completely safe action, it says, is to get inside a safe building or vehicle; tents and open-sided picnic shelters are not safe. Once inside, wait 30 minutes after the last rumble of thunder before going back out.

NWS also gives the timing. In mountain areas, it says, thunderstorms typically develop in the early afternoon, so plan to walk early in the day and be down the mountain by noon. NPS’s summer page says much the same: from May to September, a day can start with blue skies and by afternoon bring rain and snow.

Why it matters more with children

Our inference: the time that counts is not when you reach the lake but when the slowest walker is back at the car. Children walk slower on the way back, and at altitude they may feel it more. NPS says high elevation raises the risk of dehydration and mountain sickness, with headache, nausea and dizziness, and advises going down if anyone feels unwell.

The shelter also needs to be close. At Rocky Mountain, a family walking from Bear Lake at 9,475 feet (2,888 metres) may be relying on a shuttle back to the car park; in 2026, NPS ran the Bear Lake Shuttle every 10 to 15 minutes. Our inference: the wait at the stop is part of the time back, so the turnaround needs a margin.

The permit clock points the same way

At Rocky Mountain, the 2026 rules made early starts easier. NPS needed no timed entry reservation for the general park before 9 am, and said the Bear Lake car park is often full by 9 am. Bear Lake Road needed a Timed Entry+ reservation from 5 am to 6 pm, with a two-hour entry window and no re-entry until after 2 pm once you leave. NPS hadn’t posted its 2027 rules when we looked.

Four checks before a high day

1. The forecast for the top, not the town. NPS’s weather page links separate NWS forecasts for Bear Lake, Trail Ridge Road and the Alpine Visitor Center, apart from the forecast for Estes Park. Read the one for where you will be at midday.

2. A turnaround time set in the car. Pick a clock time that has everyone back at the car, or on the shuttle, by noon, and say it out loud before you set off. Our suggestion, not an NPS rule: count back from noon using the slowest walker’s pace, not the adults’.

3. The shelter you will actually use. NWS says a safe building or vehicle is the place to be; a tent, an open picnic shelter, an overhang or a cave entrance isn’t. Know which one is nearest on each walk.

4. A rule for thunder. If anyone hears thunder, the walk ends and everyone heads for the shelter, without a vote. NWS says to wait 30 minutes after the last rumble before going back out.
