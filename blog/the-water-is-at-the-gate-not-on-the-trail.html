The water is at the gate, not on the trail

In Joshua Tree National Park, the places to fill a bottle are at the entrances and in the towns, and the phone usually stops working once you’re inside. Four checks to make before a desert day, not during it.

A day in a desert national park looks simple on a plan: a short walk, a picnic, a lookout. In Joshua Tree, California, the park itself supplies none of the things that make that day safe. There is no water in its interior, nothing to buy, and very little signal. So the water, the map and the pass have to be sorted before the car reaches the gate.

The signal: the taps sit at the edges

The National Park Service (NPS) says there is no water available in the interior of Joshua Tree National Park, and no gas stations, restaurants, grocery stores or hotels anywhere in it. Its first-time visitor guide says to bring all your own food, water and supplies.

The NPS FAQ lists water bottle fill stations at the Black Rock Nature Center, the West Entrance Station and the Joshua Tree National Park Visitor Center in Twentynine Palms. Jugs can be filled at the West Entrance Station, Indian Cove Ranger Station, the Black Rock and Cottonwood campgrounds and park headquarters. None of them is in the centre of the park.

The lists don’t match exactly. The goods and services page names five water points, the safety guidelines six, and the visitor centres page lists water at Cottonwood and Black Rock but not at the Joshua Tree Visitor Center in the village, which has a café. Plan on a point that more than one page names.

How much to carry

The NPS safety guidelines say to carry at least a gallon of water per person each day. A US gallon is about 3.8 litres, so a family of four needs about 15 litres before the first walk. Its summer page goes further: four litres per person, plus extra for cooling off.

Keep a reserve in the car, too. NPS says some areas are so remote that roadside help can take time to arrive, and asks drivers whose car breaks down to stay with it until help comes.

What breaks when the plan only says “walk”

A plan that says “Barker Dam, then Keys View, then the cactus garden” assumes the family can top up, look something up or call someone between stops. NPS says cell service is extremely limited and, in many areas, nonexistent, which can make it impossible to call for help, change plans or find the rest of your group.

The pass has the same problem. NPS says digital entrance passes must be printed or saved on a phone before use, because connectivity in the park is limited or non-existent. Its FAQ says the free fourth-grade pass voucher must be printed; images on phones are not accepted.

Getting out needs a plan as well. NPS says not to rely on rideshare to get into the park, because arranging a ride out needs a signal.

Four checks before the gate

1. Water. Count the people and multiply by at least a gallon, about 3.8 litres, then add a reserve for the car. Fill up in town, not on the way past.

2. Refill point. Name the water point on today’s route before you leave: Cottonwood for a day in the south, the West Entrance or Black Rock for the west, Twentynine Palms for the north.

3. Offline. Save or print the entrance pass, download the official NPS app and its offline map, and carry a paper park map, which NPS suggests as a backup in case a phone battery dies.

4. Meeting plan. Leave your route and return time with someone outside the park, and agree a regrouping spot in case the family splits up. NPS lists 911 and 909-383-5651 for emergencies, and emergency phones at Cottonwood Visitor Center, Indian Cove Ranger Station and Intersection Rock.
