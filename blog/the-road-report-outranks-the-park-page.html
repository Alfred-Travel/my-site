The road report outranks the park page

“We’ll drive Brachina Gorge on Wednesday” sounds settled when the park page says the roads suit a high-clearance two-wheel-drive. Outback dirt roads change with the weather, and the page that describes them in general isn’t the page that describes them today. The fix is to read the dated report, and to have a sealed-road day ready.

Park pages describe a park as it usually is. In South Australia’s Ikara-Flinders Ranges National Park, the National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) page says the main road to Wilpena Pound is sealed, unsealed roads have changing surfaces that weather can affect, and all public roads are accessible by high-clearance two-wheel-drive vehicles. It then points readers to the transport department for up-to-date road conditions.

What the report said on the day we looked

The Department for Infrastructure and Transport (DIT) publishes an outback roads report and map. The report issued at 10.48 am on 18 November 2026 listed Brachina Gorge Road and Bunyeroo Valley Road, the two gorge drives NPWS names in the park, under “4WD Only (under 3 tonne / no towing)”. The report reissued at 7.05 pm on 19 November 2026 still listed both roads there.

For both roads it warned of creek crossings, scours and flagged areas, and said to follow wheel tracks through creek beds where sections of road were missing. Bunyeroo Valley Road was marked for high-clearance 4WD vehicles only. On that day, a family in an ordinary hire car would have read the park page as a yes and the report as a no.

Why the report wins

The two pages do different jobs. The park page is a standing description. The DIT report carries an issue time and says a new report will be issued whenever road conditions change. When they disagree, the dated one describes the road you will actually drive. Elsewhere in South Australia, NPWS’s closures list showed waterlogged tracks in Gawler Ranges National Park closed to all vehicles after rain.

The sign may lag too

You can’t always rely on the roadside sign to catch you. The same DIT report said that, because of a satellite outage and flood damage, some outback road signs were not updating automatically, and that signs within mobile coverage carry a QR code for the latest conditions. NPWS says mobile coverage in the park can be patchy, especially in low-lying areas, which is where creek crossings are.

DIT also runs a 24-hour automated phone service for the report, 1300 361 033, which NPWS lists on the park page. Our inference: read or call before you leave Wilpena, where there is a visitor centre and more chance of signal, rather than at the gorge turn-off.

Four checks before an unsealed drive goes on the day card

1. The report. Read the DIT outback roads report the evening before and again that morning, and note its issue time.

2. The car. Match your vehicle to the report’s category: open to all vehicles, 4WD only, or closed. Then check what your hire agreement says about unsealed roads.

3. The signal. Decide before you lose coverage. A sign may not have updated, and the QR code needs signal to work.

4. The sealed fallback. Have a day that works without the dirt road, such as walks that start from Wilpena, so a 4WD-only listing changes the plan instead of cancelling it.
