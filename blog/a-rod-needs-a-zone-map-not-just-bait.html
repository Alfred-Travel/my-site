A rod needs a zone map, not just bait

At Fly Point, near Nelson Bay, no line may go in at all. At nearby Little Beach, a line is fine from two jetties and the sand between them. Four checks to put on a family plan before the bait comes out.

Plan a trip Browse the blog

2026-11-11 · Alfred Team · Travel notes

A family fishing hour looks like the simplest item on a beach holiday: a rod, some bait, a jetty. In New South Wales it has four rules behind it, and inside a marine park the most important one depends on exactly where you stand. Port Stephens shows it clearly, because two spots on the same stretch of shore sit in different zones.

The signal: the zone sits on the water, not the jetty

NSW DPIRD says the Port Stephens–Great Lakes Marine Park runs from Cape Hawke, near Forster, south to Birubi Beach, and takes in all of Port Stephens. Its zoning map divides it into zones, including sanctuary, habitat protection and general use zones, each with its own list of what is allowed.

Fly Point, between Nelson Bay and Little Beach, is in the Fly Point–Corrie Island sanctuary zone. The zoning map marks line fishing, bait collection and shell collecting as not allowed there. Diving and snorkelling are allowed, and Visit NSW lists it as a snorkelling spot.

At Little Beach, a habitat protection zone, the map allows land-based line fishing only, from the wheelchair access jetty, the Little Beach jetty and the beach between them. No berleying and no collecting. The same map says hand picking invertebrates is not allowed in the Dutchmans Beach zone.

On a boat the rule still follows the water. DPIRD says any vessel may cross a sanctuary zone, but lines must be inboard and unbaited and other gear stowed. A boat anchored or moored in one must have its lines unrigged.

What breaks when the plan only says “fishing”

A plan that says “fishing at Nelson Bay, 4 pm” leaves the hard part to the moment the children are holding the rods. If the nearest jetty or rock is in a sanctuary zone, the hour either ends before it starts or the family fishes where it shouldn’t.

The fee has its own trap. DPIRD says it applies to anyone in possession of fishing gear in, on or next to the water, not only to someone with a line in. Its 2024–25 saltwater guide also says licences from other states and countries are not valid in NSW, so a Victorian or Queensland licence doesn’t carry over.

Who pays in a family

On 10 November 2026 DPIRD’s page listed the NSW recreational fishing fee at A$7 for three days, A$14 for a month and A$35 for a year. You can pay online through Service NSW, by phone or through fishing fee agents, and you must carry the receipt. For three days or a month no card is posted, and DPIRD says a digital receipt in the My Service NSW app counts.

Children under 18 don’t pay. Nor does an adult who is only helping a child under 18 to fish with a single rod. So a parent holding one child’s rod needs no fee, but a parent who also casts their own line does. DPIRD’s exemptions page lists the other exemptions, including Aboriginal people and some pensioner cards.

Four checks before the line goes in

1. Zone. Find the exact spot on the zoning map or in the FishSmart NSW app, which DPIRD names as the place to check zones and rules. If it’s a sanctuary zone, the plan becomes look, don’t take.

2. Fee. Count who will hold their own rod. Every adult with their own line needs the fee and the receipt on them. Children, and an adult only helping a child with one rod, don’t.

3. Limits. DPIRD says NSW bag and size limits still apply inside marine parks. Check the fish you might catch in FishSmart before deciding whether anything comes home.

4. Gear. The saltwater guide says lines must be held in the hand or fixed to the shore or a boat, and spare lines shouldn’t be rigged. One rod per child, held by a parent, keeps all four checks simple.
