An eruption forecast is a window, not a showtime

For a family flying to Hawaiʻi Island to see lava, the eruption forecast is a range of days, not a time slot. Plan several evening chances, check the observatory each day, and make the trip worth it even if the volcano stays quiet.

The USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory says Kīlauea has been erupting episodically at its summit since 23 December 2024. Fountaining episodes generally last less than 12 hours and are separated by pauses of two or more weeks. On 2 November 2026 it gave a forecast window of 4 to 7 November for the next episode.

The fix isn’t to chase the volcano. It is to read the forecast as a window that can move, give the trip more than one chance to see the eruption, and plan days that work whether or not it happens.

The signal: the window keeps moving

We read the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory’s daily Kīlauea updates through its public notice feed, and the National Park Service’s eruption viewing, current conditions, safety and weather pages for Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park, on 3 November 2026.

In the daily updates from 23 October to 2 November 2026, the forecast window for episode 56 started as 29 October to 5 November, slid later to 3 to 10 November, then narrowed to 4 to 7 November. The USGS said uncertainty remained about precise forecasting after an unusual build-up to episode 55.

The NPS says the eruption has been intermittent, with more than 50 eruptions since December 2024, that pauses last days or weeks, and that an eruption can last a few hours. Episode 55, on 17 October 2026, began at 3:19 am and ended at 9:03 am Hawaiʻi time, the USGS said.

What breaks when the forecast is treated as a showtime

One night only: if the trip has a single evening at the summit, an episode that arrives a day late, or a night of fog, is the whole chance gone. The NPS says fog or rain can block viewing, and during a pause there may be only a faint glow.

The wrong hour: episode 55 was over by 9 am. An episode that runs for a few hours can happen while children are asleep, or while the family is on the coast road with limited phone service.

No plan for the air: the NPS says volcanic gas is a hazard for infants, children, pregnant women and people with heart or breathing problems. The USGS says sulfur dioxide output during pauses typically runs from 1,000 to 5,000 tonnes a day, so air quality matters even when nothing is fountaining.

A trip that only works with lava: if every day hangs on the eruption, a paused week feels like a failed holiday. The park’s lava tube, craters, petroglyphs and coast are there either way.

Four checks for an eruption trip

Window, chances, air, backup

Read the window daily: from a week out, read the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory’s Kīlauea daily update and note how the forecast window has moved since yesterday.

Give it three chances: plan at least three evenings near the summit, with headlamps, warm layers and rain jackets, and decide each one after dinner using the update and the park webcams.

Check the air first: look at current air quality before each outing, and set a family rule for when a child with asthma stays back.

Plan the days without lava: build the daytime plan around walks and drives that are worth doing during a pause.

The four checks are our recommendation, built from the pages above. They aren’t a list the USGS or the National Park Service publishes. The NPS says cell service in the park is limited, so check the update and save your entry pass before you leave signal.

Practical next step

A week before you fly, start reading the Kīlauea daily update and write down the forecast window each day. Put at least three evening viewing chances in the plan, write the go or no-go rule on each evening card, and check the air before you leave the room.
